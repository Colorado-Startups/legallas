# Accredited investor rules

**Legallas is an AI agent for legal work, not a lawyer or law firm. Using it creates no attorney-client relationship. AI can be wrong. Have your own lawyer review before you sign, file or rely on anything. No lawyer has reviewed this.**

## In one screen

Most startup rounds are sold under Rule 506 of Regulation D, which is open to "accredited investors". The company, not
you, carries the burden: under Rule 506(b) it needs a reasonable belief that you qualify, and under Rule 506(c), where it
may advertise, it must take reasonable steps to verify that you do ([17 CFR 230.506][e506]). So expect to be asked.
This file sets out the categories, what each rule asks of you, and three fill-in forms: a self-certification, a letter
from your lawyer or accountant, and the written statements used with a high minimum investment.

## Who is accredited

The definition is in [17 CFR 230.501(a)][e501]. "Spousal equivalent" means a cohabitant in a relationship generally
equivalent to a spouse ([501(j)][e501]).

### People

| Route | Test | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Over $200,000 in each of the two most recent years, or over $300,000 jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent, and a reasonable expectation of the same this year | 501(a)(6) |
| Net worth | Over $1 million, alone or jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent, **not counting your primary residence** | 501(a)(5) |
| Professional licence | Holding in good standing a certification the SEC has designated: today the Series 7, Series 65 and Series 82 licences | 501(a)(10) |
| Insider of the issuer | A director, executive officer or general partner of the company selling, or of its general partner | 501(a)(4) |
| Knowledgeable employee | For an investment in a private fund, a knowledgeable employee of that fund | 501(a)(11) |
| Family client | A family client of a qualifying family office, when that office directs the investment | 501(a)(13) |

**How the residence is treated in net worth.** Leave the home out as an asset. Leave out the mortgage too, up to the
home's value. But debt above the home's value counts as a liability, and so does any increase in the mortgage in the 60
days before the purchase, unless it came from buying the home ([501(a)(5)(i)][e501]). Refinancing to raise cash just
before investing therefore doesn't help.

### Entities

| Route | Test | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Financial institutions and advisers | Banks, registered brokers, SEC- and state-registered investment advisers, exempt reporting advisers, insurance companies, registered funds and business development companies, and certain others | 501(a)(1) |
| Private business development company | As defined in the Advisers Act | 501(a)(2) |
| Larger entities | A charity, corporation, business trust, partnership or LLC with total assets over $5 million, **not formed to buy these securities** | 501(a)(3) |
| Trusts | Total assets over $5 million, not formed to buy these securities, directed by a sophisticated person | 501(a)(7) |
| All owners accredited | An entity in which every equity owner is accredited | 501(a)(8) |
| Other entities with investments | Any other kind of entity owning over $5 million in "investments" (defined in [17 CFR 270.2a51-1(b)][e2a51]), not formed to buy these securities | 501(a)(9) |
| Family offices | Assets under management over $5 million, not formed to buy these securities, directed by a person with the needed experience | 501(a)(12) |

A newly formed special purpose vehicle usually can't meet the $5 million tests, so it depends on every member being
accredited under 501(a)(8).

## What each rule asks of you

| | Rule 506(b) | Rule 506(c) |
|---|---|---|
| Can the company advertise? | No general solicitation | Yes |
| Who may buy | Accredited investors, plus up to 35 sophisticated non-accredited purchasers across the company's Rule 506(b) sales in any 90-day period | Accredited investors only |
| What the company needs | A reasonable belief that you are accredited | Reasonable steps to verify that you are |
| What you will usually give | A signed questionnaire or certification (Form 1 below) | Documents, a letter from a professional (Form 2), or written statements with a high minimum (Form 3) |

### The listed ways to verify under Rule 506(c)

Rule 506(c)(2)(ii) lists ways that count as reasonable steps. They are not the only ways, but they are safe.

1. **Income:** IRS forms reporting your income for the two most recent years (such as Forms W-2, 1099, Schedule K-1 or
   Form 1040), plus your written statement that you expect to qualify this year.
2. **Net worth:** statements dated within the prior three months showing assets (bank, brokerage, appraisal), plus a
   consumer credit report from a nationwide agency for liabilities, plus your written statement that you disclosed all
   liabilities.
3. **A letter** from a registered broker-dealer, an investment adviser registered with the SEC (a state-registered
   adviser isn't on the list), a licensed lawyer or a certified public accountant, saying they took reasonable steps to verify your status within the prior three months.
4. **Earlier verification by the same company:** if the company verified you before, it may rely on your written
   statement at the time of sale that you still qualify, for five years from the earlier verification, unless it knows
   otherwise. A verification by a different company doesn't count.

### The high minimum route (a staff view)

In a 2025 letter, SEC staff agreed that a high minimum investment is a relevant factor, and that a company could
reasonably conclude it took reasonable steps to verify where three things are true: the investor states in writing that
it is accredited (naming the category); the investor states in writing that the minimum isn't financed, in whole or in
part, by a third party for the specific purpose of making this investment; and the company has no actual knowledge of
facts pointing the other way ([staff letter][latham]). The staff's reply refers to the minimum amounts "described in"
the request letter without restating them; commentary reports those as $200,000 for a natural person and $1 million for
an entity. For an entity accredited only because all its owners are accredited, the no-financing statement covers each
owner's share too. This is staff guidance, not a rule, and it has no legal force.

## Form 1: Accredited investor certification

For a Rule 506(b) round, or as the starting point for a Rule 506(c) round. Check every box that applies.

> **Accredited investor certification**
>
> To: {{company_name}} (the "Company")
>
> I, {{signer_name}}, certify for {{investor_name}} (the "Investor") that, as of the date below, the Investor is an
> "accredited investor" as defined in Rule 501(a) of Regulation D under the Securities Act of 1933, because the
> Investor meets the category or categories checked below.
>
> **If the Investor is a natural person:**
>
> - [ ] Individual income over $200,000 in each of the two most recent years, or joint income with a spouse or spousal
>   equivalent over $300,000 in each of those years, and a reasonable expectation of reaching the same level this year.
> - [ ] Net worth, alone or with a spouse or spousal equivalent, over $1,000,000, calculated without the primary
>   residence and as Rule 501(a)(5) requires for debt secured by it.
> - [ ] Holder in good standing of a Series 7, Series 65 or Series 82 licence (licence number: ________).
> - [ ] A director, executive officer or general partner of the Company, or of its general partner.
>
> **If the Investor is an entity:**
>
> - [ ] Total assets over $5,000,000, and not formed for the specific purpose of acquiring these securities.
>   Kind of entity: {{investor_type}}.
> - [ ] Every equity owner is an accredited investor. (Attach a list of owners and each owner's category.)
> - [ ] Owns investments, as defined in Rule 2a51-1(b) under the Investment Company Act, over $5,000,000, and not formed
>   for the specific purpose of acquiring these securities.
> - [ ] Another category of Rule 501(a), stated here: {{category}}.
>
> The Investor will tell the Company promptly in writing if this certification stops being true before the Investor's
> purchase closes. The Investor understands that the Company will rely on this certification to decide whether it may
> sell securities to the Investor.
>
> Signed: ____________________  Name and title: {{signer_name}}  Date: {{sign_date}}

## Form 2: Verification letter from a lawyer or accountant

For a Rule 506(c) round. Your own professional reviews your documents and gives the company this letter, so the company
never holds your tax returns. The professional decides what review is enough; this is only the letter.

> **Verification of accredited investor status**
>
> To: {{company_name}}
>
> I am {{verifier_name}}, a [licensed attorney / certified public accountant / registered broker-dealer / investment
> adviser registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission], licence or registration {{verifier_licence}}.
>
> Within the three months before the date of this letter, I took reasonable steps to verify that {{investor_name}} is an
> accredited investor as defined in Rule 501(a) of Regulation D, and I determined that {{investor_name}} is an
> accredited investor under the following category: {{category}}. I completed my review on {{review_date}}.
>
> This letter is given for the Company's use in determining whether it may sell securities to {{investor_name}} under
> Rule 506(c). It is not an opinion on any other matter.
>
> Signed: ____________________  Date: ________

## Form 3: Written statements for a high minimum investment

For a Rule 506(c) round where the investment is at or above the minimum described in the staff letter. Give it with
Form 1.

> **Investor statements**
>
> To: {{company_name}}
>
> {{investor_name}} states that:
>
> 1. It is an accredited investor as defined in Rule 501(a) of Regulation D, under the category stated in its
>    certification of the same date.
> 2. Its investment of {{amount}} is not financed, in whole or in part, by any third party for the specific purpose of
>    making this investment.
> 3. *(Only if the Investor is an entity accredited because all its equity owners are accredited.)* No equity owner's
>    share of this investment is financed, in whole or in part, by any third party for the specific purpose of making
>    this investment.
>
> Signed: ____________________  Name and title: {{signer_name}}  Date: {{sign_date}}

## Drafting notes

### Choice: how to show status in a Rule 506(c) round

| Option | Privacy | Cost and effort | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand over tax forms or account statements | Low: the company keeps copies of personal documents | None in money | The listed method; documents must be recent for net worth |
| A letter from your own lawyer or accountant (Form 2) | High: the company sees only the letter | A professional's fee | The listed method; good for three months |
| High minimum with written statements (Form 3) | High | None | Staff view only; works only above the minimum |
| A verification service | Medium: the service holds your documents | A fee, often paid by the company | Not a listed method in itself; the company must still be satisfied the steps were reasonable |

**Recommendation:** if your check is at or above $200,000 (or $1 million for an entity), offer Form 3 with Form 1. If not,
use a letter from your own accountant or lawyer, so your tax returns stay with someone who owes you confidentiality.

**Where a lawyer should look:** whether the company's process meets "reasonable steps" for its offering. That is the
company's risk, but a failed exemption can unwind the round you are in.

### Choice: entity investing

**Recommendation:** if you invest through a trust, LLC or family entity, check the "not formed for the specific purpose"
condition first. An entity formed for this deal usually has to rely on every owner being accredited.

**Where a lawyer should look:** trusts (whether the trust or its grantor is the right investor) and family offices.
