# How members invest

**Legallas is an AI agent for legal work, not a lawyer or law firm. Using it creates no attorney-client relationship. AI can be wrong. Have your own lawyer review before you sign, file or rely on anything. No lawyer has reviewed this.** Have your own lawyer look before you set
up any vehicle or pay anyone.

## In one screen

An angel group's members can invest in four ways: each member directly; through a one-deal vehicle (often called a
special purpose vehicle, or a syndicate when a lead invites backers into it); through a pooled fund; or a mix, such as a
network whose members invest directly alongside a "sidecar" fund. **The choice decides which securities laws reach the
group.** Members investing directly, with no one paid, keep the group mostly outside broker, adviser and fund rules. A
pooled vehicle is itself an issuer of securities and usually a private fund, so it needs an Investment Company Act
exclusion, an offering exemption and a Form D, and its manager needs an answer on adviser registration. Paying anyone
on a deal raises the broker question. Colorado has no series LLC statute, so a Colorado group that wants one entity with
a series per deal must use Delaware law.

No SEC or Colorado statement written for angel groups on direct versus pooled investing was found. The nearest official
guidance is the SEC's investor bulletin on investment clubs.

## The four ways, side by side

| Question | Members invest directly | One-deal vehicle (syndicate) | Pooled fund | Network with a sidecar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who appears on the startup's capitalization table | Each member | The vehicle | The fund | Members, and the sidecar |
| Is the group or vehicle selling a security? | The group usually isn't. Each startup sells its own | Yes: interests in the vehicle | Yes: interests in the fund | The sidecar is |
| Investment Company Act exclusion needed? | No, the group holds no securities | Yes, usually 3(c)(1) | Yes | For the sidecar |
| Form D | Each startup files its own | The vehicle files too | The fund files | The sidecar files |
| Adviser question | Arises if someone is paid to recommend deals | The manager is advising a private fund | Same | Same, for the sidecar manager |
| Broker question | Arises if anyone is paid on deals | Arises if the manager is paid on the deal | Arises for fees tied to raising money | Both |
| Tax | Each member's own records | A Schedule K-1 per vehicle per year | One K-1 per year | Both |
| Who holds the startup's information and voting rights | Each member | The manager | The manager | Both |

## The broker trap

**The rule.** A broker is anyone "engaged in the business of effecting transactions in securities for the account of
others" ([15 U.S.C. 78c(a)(4)][us78c]), and a broker must register with the SEC unless an exemption applies
([78o(a)][us78o]). Colorado separately requires a broker-dealer or sales representative to be licensed
([C.R.S. 11-51-201, 11-51-401][t11]). SEC staff have long described pay tied to the success of a transaction as a
hallmark of broker activity, and their broker registration guide addresses finders ([SEC broker guide][sec-bd], staff
view; the guide's own wording couldn't be opened from this session).

**The federal carve-out.** The JOBS Act added a carve-out, now at 15 U.S.C. 77d(c) (the JOBS Act and the SEC's FAQ
call it section 4(b)). For Rule 506 offerings, a person does not have to register as a broker solely because it runs a
platform or mechanism for offers and sales, co-invests, or provides "ancillary services": due diligence without
separately paid advice, and standard documents, if it doesn't negotiate terms for others. It applies only **if** the
person and its associated persons receive no compensation in connection with the purchase or sale, don't hold
investors' money or securities, and aren't disqualified ([15 U.S.C. 77d(c)][us77d]). It lifts only the federal
registration requirement.

**What staff said "compensation" means.** In a 2013 FAQ, SEC staff read compensation broadly, as any direct or indirect
economic benefit, but not the profit a person makes on its own co-investment ([SEC FAQ][sec-jobs], staff view, read
through secondary copies). The FAQ also says the carve-out doesn't exempt anyone from state broker registration; the
statute itself says nothing about state law.

**How it lands on a group.**

| Who is paid, and how | Plain reading |
|---|---|
| No one is paid; members share costs | Fits the carve-out most cleanly |
| Dues pay for meeting costs only | Probably fine, but dues are an economic benefit to whoever receives them; a lawyer should look |
| Dues fund staff who screen, introduce or help close deals | Needs a lawyer: staff pay could be compensation "in connection with" sales |
| The group charges companies to apply or present | Needs a lawyer: a company-paid fee is the clearest case of compensation. Keep any fee small, flat and unrelated to whether the company raises money |
| Anyone receives a percentage of money raised, or a success fee | Treat as a broker activity unless a registered broker does it |
| A lead takes carried interest on a syndicate | SEC staff letters in 2013 allowed carry-only pay on their own facts, for an adviser charging no management fee ([AngelList letter][angellist]). Those facts may not match yours |

**Colorado.** No Colorado exemption matching section 4(c) was found, and no Colorado statement on finders. A person paid
on Colorado deals should get a Colorado answer separately.

## The adviser trap

**The rule.** An investment adviser is anyone who, for compensation, is in the business of advising others about
securities ([15 U.S.C. 80b-2(a)(11)][us80b2]); Colorado's definition is similar ([C.R.S. 11-51-201][t11]). A member
investing their own money is not an adviser on that account. A person paid to pick or recommend deals for others may be.
The SEC's investment club bulletin makes the same point: a person paid for advice, or one member making choices for
others, may be an adviser ([SEC investment club bulletin][sec-clubs], read through secondary copies).

**Vehicles make it concrete.** A vehicle relying on 3(c)(1) is a "private fund" ([80b-2(a)(29)][us80b2]), and whoever is
paid to manage it advises a private fund. The federal routes:

| Route | Who fits | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Venture capital fund adviser ([80b-3(l)][us80b3], [Rule 203(l)-1][e203l]) | Advises only funds that meet the rule's venture capital fund test, at any size | Exempt reporting adviser: Form ADV filed within 60 days, then yearly; no brochure |
| Private fund adviser under $150 million ([80b-3(m)][us80b3], [Rule 203(m)-1][e203m]) | Advises only private funds, with under $150 million in the United States | Same reports |
| State registration | Below the federal thresholds, and not exempt | State licence and its rules |

**Colorado.** Since 2026-08-12, anyone transacting business in Colorado as an investment adviser needs a Colorado
licence or an exemption, whether or not it has an office here; the old "place of business in this state" limit was
removed ([C.R.S. 11-51-401(1.5)][t11]). The statute has no private fund exemption. Colorado's rule supplies one: an
adviser solely to private funds is exempt if neither it nor an advisory affiliate has a bad actor event, it files with
the state each exempt reporting adviser report it files with the SEC, and it pays the fee the Securities Commissioner
sets. An adviser to a 3(c)(1) fund that isn't a venture capital fund must also limit that fund to qualified clients
(net worth measured without the primary residence), give each investor written disclosures, and deliver audited
financial statements every year ([3 CCR 704-1, Rule 51-4.11(IA)][ccr]).

**Delaware.** An adviser with no place of business in Delaware needs no Delaware registration if its only Delaware
clients are institutions, or it had no more than 5 other Delaware clients in the past 12 months
([6 Del. C. 73-301(c)][d73s3]). Delaware's private fund adviser exemption excludes every 3(c)(1) fund, venture funds
included ([Delaware Securities Act Rule 711][derules]), so a manager with a Delaware office advising 3(c)(1) vehicles
has no rule exemption. Whether a vehicle formed in Delaware, or its investors, count as Delaware clients is unsettled.
This is a lawyer point.

**A one-deal vehicle and the venture test.** To count as a venture capital fund, a vehicle must buy mainly directly from
the company, and may hold no more than 20% of its capital in non-qualifying investments such as shares bought from
existing holders ([Rule 203(l)-1][e203l]). A vehicle formed to buy shares from a departing founder may fail the test.

## The fund trap

A vehicle that holds securities is an investment company unless excluded. The usual exclusion is 3(c)(1): no more than
100 beneficial owners, or 250 for a qualifying venture capital fund with no more than $12 million of capital, and no
public offering ([15 U.S.C. 80a-3(c)(1)][us80a3], [Rule 3c-7][e3c7]). If another fund owns 10% or more of the vehicle's
voting securities, that fund's owners may be counted ([80a-3(c)(1)(A)][us80a3]). A public website inviting anyone to join
a vehicle can be a public offering.

## The offering trap

Interests in a vehicle are securities. The SEC's club bulletin warns: "If the club has even one passive member, it may be
issuing securities" ([SEC investment club bulletin][sec-clubs], read through secondary copies). So each vehicle needs its own offering exemption (usually
Rule 506(b) or (c)), files its own Form D within 15 days after its first sale ([Rule 503][e503]), makes its own state
notice filings, and runs its own bad actor check on its manager ([Rule 506(d)][e506]).

Under Colorado law, a seller who makes an untrue statement or misleading omission is liable to the buyer unless it proves
it couldn't have known with reasonable care, and anyone who knowingly gives substantial help to such a violation can be
liable too ([C.R.S. 11-51-604(4), (5)(c)][t11]). A syndicate lead who presents a deal to backers should treat what it
says as carrying that risk.

Two counting rules matter:

- **Accreditation of the vehicle.** A new vehicle usually can't show $5 million in assets, so it relies on every owner
  being accredited ([Rule 501(a)(8)][e501]).
- **The startup's purchaser count.** Under Rule 506(b), a vehicle counts as one purchaser, unless it was formed to buy
  these securities and isn't accredited under 501(a)(8), in which case each owner counts ([Rule 501(e)(2)][e501]). A
  vehicle with non-accredited members can use up the startup's 35 places.

## Pitch events: Rule 148

A group can host companies at a demo day without it being general solicitation by the companies, if Rule 148's
conditions are met: more than one issuer; no advertising of a specific offering; the sponsor gives no investment advice,
doesn't negotiate for investors, charges attendees no more than reasonable administrative fees, and takes no pay for
introductions or pay that would require broker or adviser registration; the companies say no more about their offerings than that
they are raising, the type and amount of securities, the intended use of proceeds and the unsubscribed amount; and online
attendance is limited to the group's members, people reasonably believed accredited, or
people invited on disclosed experience criteria ([17 CFR 230.148][e148]). The rule defines an "angel investor group" as
accredited investors who hold regular meetings and have defined processes and procedures for making
investment decisions (individually or as a group), and who are not associated or affiliated with brokers, dealers or investment advisers. What "associated or
affiliated" means for a group whose manager also runs a sidecar fund is unsettled.

## Colorado has no series LLC, and what that means for syndicate vehicles

The Colorado Limited Liability Company Act ([C.R.S. 7-80][t7]) has no series provision; the word "series" doesn't appear
in it. Bills to add one died in committee in 2020 and 2021 (design map, from the legislature's bill pages). So a Colorado LLC can't hold deal-by-deal
series with separate liability under Colorado law. Delaware can: a **protected series** is set up in the LLC agreement,
with separate records and notice of the liability limit in the certificate of formation ([6 Del. C. 18-215][d18s2]); a
**registered series** is formed by filing a certificate with the Delaware Secretary of State ([18-218][d18s2]) for $110
([fee schedule][defee], read 2026-11-06, still the schedule the fee page links), and pays its own annual tax ([18-1107][d18s11]).

| Option for one vehicle per deal | Cost and work | Liability between deals | Colorado points |
|---|---|---|---|
| A separate Colorado LLC for each deal | A Colorado formation and periodic report for each | Separate entities, clean | Simple and home-state; paperwork grows with every deal |
| A separate Delaware LLC for each deal | A Delaware formation and annual tax for each, plus Colorado authority if it transacts business here ([C.R.S. 7-90-801][t7]) | Separate entities, clean | Two states' fees per deal |
| One Delaware LLC with a protected series per deal | One entity filing; each series is created in the agreement | Depends on keeping separate records for each series | Colorado law sends a foreign entity's internal affairs and owner liability to its home state's law ([7-90-805(4)][t7]), and two Colorado definitions count a foreign protected series as a person ([C.R.S. 4-1-201(b)(26)][t4]; 11-111-102(11)), but no Colorado text names the shield between series. Whether a Colorado court respects it is unsettled |
| One Delaware LLC with a registered series per deal | A certificate and annual tax for each series | As above, with a public filing per series | Same open question |
| A platform's existing vehicle structure | The platform's fees and terms | The platform's structure, often Delaware series | The platform runs the filings; read its agreement |

**Recommendation:** for a few deals a year, form a separate LLC for each deal, in Colorado if the manager and most members
are in Colorado. The extra filings cost little next to the risk of an untested series shield. Consider a Delaware series
only at a volume where per-deal formation becomes a real burden, and then have a lawyer advise on Colorado's treatment.

## Drafting notes

### Choice: which model the group uses: `{{model}}`

| Option | Good when | Main legal cost |
|---|---|---|
| Members invest directly | Members want control and their own tax records; no one is paid | Each member signs each deal; the startup handles many investors |
| One-deal vehicles | Small checks pooled into one line on the capitalization table | Form D, state notices, K-1s and an adviser answer for every vehicle |
| Pooled fund | Members want diversification and will accept a manager's choices | Full fund formation; see [Fund formation](../funds/fund-formation.md) |
| Network with a sidecar | Mix of active and passive members | Both sets of costs, and the Rule 148 "affiliated" question |

**Recommendation:** start with members investing directly and no one paid on deals. Add one-deal vehicles only when a
manager is ready to file Form ADV as an exempt reporting adviser, Form D for each vehicle, and Colorado's exemption
filing. Choose a pooled fund only with a lawyer engaged from the start.

**Where a lawyer should look:** the manager's pay (`{{manager_pay}}`) against the broker carve-out and Colorado law;
the adviser route; the Rule 148 "associated or affiliated" question if the group hosts demo days and also runs a
vehicle.

### Choice: who manages a vehicle, and how they are paid

**Recommendation:** pay the manager only through carried interest and a modest, disclosed fee for actual costs, never
through fees from the companies or a share of money raised. Even then, file as an exempt reporting adviser if the manager
is paid for managing the vehicle.

**Where a lawyer should look:** whether a management fee changes the broker analysis; Colorado licensing for the manager
and anyone else paid.

### Choice: tax effect on members

A member gets the qualified small business stock benefit through a vehicle only if the member held the interest on the
date the vehicle bought the stock, and only for the share held then ([26 U.S.C. 1202(g)][us1202]). **Recommendation:**
admit all members of a one-deal vehicle before it buys, and never admit members after.
