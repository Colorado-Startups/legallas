# Colorado LLC, Colorado C corporation or Delaware C corporation

> Legallas is an AI agent for legal work, not a lawyer or law firm. Using it creates no attorney-client relationship. AI can be wrong. Have your own lawyer review before you sign, file or rely on anything.
>
> No lawyer has reviewed this comparison. Fees and rules change, so every figure carries its date.

This compares three forms for a company run from Colorado with people working in Colorado. A Delaware LLC has its own
[comparison](delaware-llc-comparison.md).

"C corporation" isn't a kind of company a state forms. It's the federal tax treatment any domestic corporation has unless it
elects S status. So a corporation formed in Colorado is a C corporation in the same way one formed in Delaware is.

**Three terms used below.** *Qualified small business stock* is original-issue stock in a small C corporation whose holder can exclude some or
all of the gain from federal income tax after holding it for at least three years, if the tests in [26 U.S.C. 1202][us1202] are met. *Foreign
authority* is the Colorado filing that registers an out-of-state company to do business here. *Advancement* means the company pays a person's
legal costs while a case is running, not only after it ends.

## Recommendation by situation

These are recommendations, not rules. Start with the first of rows 1 to 4 that fits. Rows 5 to 7 apply on top of that choice. The [product notices](../../docs/legal/product-notices.md), section A4, limit when the product may show them: a surface without the user's own lawyer connected shows the options and factors below, and not this table.

| Your situation | Recommendation | Why | Where a lawyer should look |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. You expect to raise a priced round from institutional venture funds within about two years, or you're applying to a program that asks for a Delaware company | **Delaware C corporation**, plus Colorado foreign authority | The standard financing forms are written for Delaware, and that's what companies raising large rounds use: among Colorado-based technology and life science companies in SEC data, 70 of the 75 that reported $5 million or more sold were Delaware corporations and 1 was a Colorado corporation. That shows habit, not cause. The extra state cost is about $500 a year at the start, and it grows with assets | Whether your target investors truly require it; franchise tax method; investor protective terms |
| 2. You're raising on SAFEs (simple agreements for future equity) or notes from angels, friends and family, with no priced round in sight | **Colorado C corporation**, with the [consent and cumulative voting clauses](colorado-corporation-articles.md) | Lowest state cost for a corporation. Same federal treatment, same Colorado income tax, same qualified small business stock clock from day one. Conversion later keeps the entity and the clock. 38 Colorado corporations in that data raised money in two years, 8 of them $1 million or more | Whether the articles fit your cap table; how an investor's counsel would treat conversion at a priced round |
| 3. You're not sure whether a fund will ever invest | **A Colorado C corporation, converting if a fund asks** | A corporation starts the qualified small business stock clock and can grant incentive stock options. An LLC taxed as a partnership can do neither. Between the two states, Delaware's cost is certain and yearly, about $2,660 over five years. Colorado's is a one-time conversion that happens only if money is arriving: from $443 in state fees, plus a lawyer's time, whose cost nobody has measured, and an investor reaction nobody has measured either. If a real fund has already shown interest, this becomes row 1 | How an investor's counsel would treat it; tax on your specific plan |
| 4. You want profits paid out, no venture funds, or owners who can use early losses: a services, real estate or family business | **Colorado LLC** | Pass-through tax by default, $25 a year, and a flexible operating agreement that can set almost any governance terms | Self-employment tax; whether units are securities; the operating agreement's removal and buy-sell terms |
| 5. You and a co-founder hold equal stakes and fear deadlock, or you'd be the smaller holder and fear being pushed out | **Sign the [founders' agreement](founders-agreement.md), whichever form you chose.** The state doesn't settle this | It's mixed. Colorado gives a 10% meeting demand and a court route for oppression. But the buyout there is the other side's option, and it can hand them a right to buy you out. Delaware has no holding-period gate on records and lists board materials. Contracts do more than either statute. See [when founders disagree](#when-founders-disagree) | Every clause that decides who can remove whom |
| 6. You'll control the company and want to act fast by majority, including replacing directors | **Delaware**, or Colorado with both opt-ins | Delaware's default lets the needed majority act by consent with no prior notice. Colorado gets close only if the articles say so and cumulative voting is off | Duties owed when a controller acts; notice afterward |
| 7. You're in law, health, consulting, finance, farming, hospitality or another excluded line | Decide without counting on qualified small business stock | Those lines are outside section 1202 whichever state you choose | Your own tax professional, on [26 U.S.C. 1202(e)(3)][us1202] |

## The claim being tested

There are two claims, and both were tested the same way.

**The standard advice:** form a Delaware C corporation.
**The challenge to it:** many Colorado founders don't know a Colorado C corporation is an option, and it may be better.

**The verdict.** The challenge is right that the option is real and cheaper, and that Colorado's statute gives a smaller holder
more tools on some points. It isn't shown to be better in general. The standard advice is right for a company headed to institutional venture money,
mostly for reasons of market habit and documents rather than law or tax. It's weaker for a Colorado-run company that may never
take fund money. Nobody has measured what founders know, or how often investors actually require a conversion.

## Cost to form and keep up

State fees only, excluding lawyers and income tax. Colorado and Delaware figures are as read on 2026-11-01 by the
[document map's research][map]. They weren't reopened for this comparison: the Colorado Secretary of State's terms bar robots.
Delaware's fee page (corp.delaware.gov/fee/) answers a plain fetch (2026-11-06), but its figures weren't reread here. Check both before you rely on a number.

| | Colorado LLC | Colorado C corporation | Delaware C corporation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form | $50 | $50 | From $109, plus $100 Colorado foreign authority |
| Yearly report | $25 | $25 | $50 Delaware report: "a fee of $50" for a corporation that isn't exempt ([8 Del. C. 391(a)(18)][d8s18], read 2026-11-07), plus $25 Colorado report |
| Franchise tax | None | None | Lesser of two methods; see below |
| Registered agent | The company itself can serve if it has a usual place of business in Colorado ([7-90-701][t7]) | Same | A Delaware agent, about $50 a year on one vendor's quote, plus Colorado (the company can serve) |
| **Typical yearly total** | **$25** | **$25** | **About $525:** $400 tax, $75 reports, $50 agent |
| Colorado income tax | Pass-through by default | 4.40% ([39-22-301][t39]) | **The same 4.40%** |
| Delaware income tax | None | None | None for a corporation keeping only a statutory office there ([30 Del. C. 1902][d30]). Gross receipts tax is for businesses operating in Delaware, and withholding for employees working there ([Division FAQ][defaq]) |

**The Delaware tax scare.** Under [8 Del. C. 503][d8c5], the tax is the lesser of two methods, but only if you report issued shares
and gross assets. Otherwise you pay on authorized shares. For a company with 10 million authorized and 8 million issued shares:
$85,165 on authorized shares, and $400 at $100,000 of gross assets on the Division's [assumed par value capital method][defrcalc]
(calculated here; that method's statutory minimum is $400 ([503(c)][d8c5])). File the report with the numbers. The tax on that method
grows with assets: the same company at $5 million of gross assets owes $2,800.

**The statute's own words** ([8 Del. C. 503][d8c5], read 2026-11-07):

| Method | Words |
|---|---|
| Authorized shares | "where the authorized capital stock does not exceed 5,000 shares, $175", then $250 up to 10,000 shares and $85 for each further 10,000 ([503(a)(1)][d8c5]) |
| Assumed par value | "add $400 for each $1,000,000 or fraction thereof in excess of $1,000,000" of assumed par value capital ([503(a)(2)][d8c5]) |
| Cap and floor | Neither method's tax for a full year may "be more than $200,000"; the floors are $175 and $400 ([503(c)][d8c5]) |

**Five years, calculated here:** at the $400 tax, Delaware costs about $2,660 more than Colorado: about $500 a year, plus $159 more to
start.

**Skipping Colorado foreign authority** isn't a saving. A Delaware company transacting business here can't sue to collect debts in
Colorado courts until it files. It also owes fees of up to $100 a year and faces a civil penalty of up to $5,000 ([7-90-802][t7]).

## Voting and written consent

| Question | Colorado C corporation | Delaware C corporation | Colorado LLC |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Owners acting without a meeting, by default** | Everyone entitled to vote must consent ([7-107-104(1)(a)][t7]) | Holders of the votes needed at a full meeting may act, with no prior notice ([228(a)][d8s7]) | No statutory procedure. Decisions are by majority, with listed acts needing everyone ([7-80-401][t7]). The agreement must supply the mechanics |
| **Can that change?** | Yes. The articles must **expressly** allow the needed votes ([7-107-104(1)(b)][t7]). Put it in the original articles | Yes, the certificate can restrict it | Yes, in the operating agreement ([7-80-108][t7]) |
| **Notice to those who didn't sign** | Upon receipt of the last consent, to holders entitled to vote who didn't sign ([7-107-104(5.5)][t7]) | Prompt notice afterward ([228(e)][d8s7]) | As the agreement says. Our template requires it |
| **Time limit for gathering consents** | 60 days from the first one received ([7-107-104(2)][t7]) | 60 days from the first one delivered ([228(c)][d8s7]) | As the agreement says |
| **Cumulative voting** | **On** unless the articles say otherwise ([7-102-102(3)][t7]) | Off unless the certificate adopts it ([214][d8s7]) | Doesn't apply |
| **The trap** | With cumulative voting on, electing or removing directors by consent needs **every** holder, even if the articles allow majority consent ([7-107-104(1.5)][t7]). So the two clauses work together | A less-than-unanimous consent to elect directors replaces the annual meeting only if all directorships are vacant and filled by that action ([211(b)][d8s7]) | None |
| **Removing a director** | Votes for must exceed votes against, at a meeting called for that purpose ([7-108-108][t7]). With cumulative voting on, enough votes against to have elected the director block it. The articles may limit removal to cause ([7-108-108(1)][t7]) | Majority of voting shares, with or without cause, and by consent. Cause-only applies only with a classified board or cumulative voting ([141(k)][d8s4]) | Managers: consent of a majority of members ([7-80-402][t7]). The statute doesn't say whether by head or by stake; the agreement should ([7-80-706][t7]) |
| **Board acting without a meeting** | All directors ([7-108-202][t7]) | All directors ([141(f)][d8s4]) | Majority of managers or members decide; the agreement sets the mechanics ([7-80-401][t7]) |
| **Amending the charter** | Votes for exceed votes against, with a quorum ([7-110-103, 7-107-206][t7]) | Majority of outstanding stock ([242][d8s8]) | **Every member**, unless the agreement says otherwise ([7-80-401(2)][t7]) |
| **Separate class votes** | Wide list, including a new class prior or equal, and it applies even to nonvoting shares ([7-110-104][t7]). No opt-out in the text | Narrower list; a vote on the authorized number can be opted out ([242(b)(2)][d8s8]) | As the agreement says |
| **Stockholder contracts limiting the board** | Voting agreements ([7-107-302][t7]), transfer agreements ([7-106-208][t7]), and limits on the board in the articles ([7-102-102(2)(b)(III)][t7], [7-107-208][t7]). No counterpart to 122(18) found, so a side contract binding the board is untested | Voting agreements ([218][d8s7]) and contracts giving approval rights ([122(18)][d8s2]) | The operating agreement can carry all of it |

**A reading to check.** [7-108-108(4)][t7] says shareholders may remove a director "only at a meeting called for the purpose". But
[7-107-104(1)][t7] lets any meeting action be taken by consent unless the articles require a meeting, and subsection (1.5) expressly
speaks of removing directors by consent. Read together, consent appears to work, but no Colorado appellate decision on this point
was found. A lawyer should confirm it before anyone relies on it.

**How cumulative voting works.** Each share gets one vote per open seat, and a holder can stack them. To be sure of one seat you need
more than total shares divided by (seats + 1). On a three-seat board that's just over 25%. So a founder with 26% can elect a seat while three seats are up together, though
the bylaws set board size ([7-108-103(1)][t7]) and the articles can stagger terms ([7-108-106][t7]), and either can defeat it. An investor promised seats
may not get what the deal says unless the articles opt out.

## When founders disagree

These are the same rules, read for common disputes: founders disagreeing, a founder being removed, a board or investors acting on a
founder's conduct, and a contest for control. Most of what decides them is in the contracts signed at formation and at the raise,
not the state chosen. See the [founders' agreement](founders-agreement.md) and the [founder stock agreement](founder-stock-purchase.md).

| Question | Colorado C corporation | Delaware C corporation | Colorado LLC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can the founder be fired as an officer? | Board, at any time, with or without cause, unless bylaws differ ([7-108-303(4)][t7]). Contract rights survive ([7-108-304][t7]) | Officers serve as bylaws or the board provide; no stated vote ([142][d8s4]). Our template bylaws state it | Majority of members, or of managers ([7-80-403][t7]) |
| Can the founder lose a board seat? | See removal above. A court can also remove for fraud, dishonesty or gross abuse, on a suit by the company or 10% of any class ([7-108-109][t7]) | See removal above. Court removal is far narrower ([225(c)][d8s7]) | A manager: majority of members, unless the agreement says otherwise ([7-80-402][t7]). In a member-run LLC, no statutory right to expel a member was found, so the agreement decides |
| How fast can it happen? | Without the opt-ins: a meeting on 10 to 60 days' notice ([7-107-105][t7]), or everyone's signature. With them: consent, with notice afterward, if consent can remove a director at all ([see above](#voting-and-written-consent)) | Same day, by consent, with notice afterward ([228][d8s7]). A founder can learn of it after the fact | As the agreement says. Our template requires notice first |
| Can a holder force a meeting? | Yes: 10% of the votes can demand a special meeting ([7-107-102(1)(b)][t7]), and a court can order it ([7-107-103][t7]) | Only the board or persons named in the certificate or bylaws can call one ([211(d)][d8s7]). A court can order an overdue annual meeting ([211(c)][d8s7]) | No statute; the agreement decides |
| Can a removed founder see the records? | Basics on 5 business days' written demand. Minutes, accounting records and the shareholder list on 3 months' holding or 5%, good faith and a proper purpose. Articles and bylaws can't limit this ([7-116-102(5)][t7]). If a court orders inspection, it must award costs and counsel fees unless the company proves a good-faith refusal ([7-116-104(3)][t7]) | Sworn demand and proper purpose, with no holding period. A defined list narrowed in 2025 that includes board minutes and materials ([220][d8s7]). The Court of Chancery has exclusive jurisdiction ([220(c)][d8s7]), so a Colorado founder goes to Delaware | Written demand with a related purpose; the agreement can't unreasonably restrict it ([7-80-408, -108(2)(b)][t7]) |
| Is there a remedy for being squeezed out? | **Yes, by statute, with a catch.** A shareholder may seek dissolution if those in control act illegally, oppressively or fraudulently ([7-114-301(2)(b)][t7]). The company or other holders may elect to buy the petitioner's shares at fair value instead, unless the articles or bylaws say otherwise, and once they elect, the petitioner can't settle or sell without court approval ([7-114-305][t7]). A Colorado court has described oppression as burdensome, harsh and wrongful conduct ([Polk][polk]), and a fiduciary breach can show it ([Colt][colt]) | **No oppression ground in the statute.** Claims run through fiduciary duty, which is judge-made. Custodian on deadlock ([226][d8s7]); 50/50 joint ventures ([273][d8s10]) | Only if it's not reasonably practicable to carry on ([7-80-810(2)][t7]). The Court of Appeals refused to read in the corporate oppression ground, and it treats misconduct as one factor ([Gagne 2014][gagne]). A later decision in the same dispute affirmed dissolving viable LLCs on findings including misconduct and oppression ([Gagne 2019][gagne2]). A member may resign but keeps only the economic share ([7-80-602, -603][t7]) |
| Deadlock with 50/50 holders? | Dissolution if directors or holders are deadlocked and listed harm follows, with the buyout election ([7-114-301(2)(a), (c)][t7]) | Custodian ([226][d8s7]), or dissolution for a two-holder joint venture ([273][d8s10]) | Reasonably practicable test above |
| Can investors add a senior class over the common's objection? | Common votes as a separate group on a new class prior or equal, even if nonvoting ([7-110-104(1)(f), (4)][t7]). Board-set series under [7-106-102][t7] are the likely route around it, if the articles allow them. A lawyer should confirm | Creating a new senior class isn't on the class-vote list ([242(b)(2)][d8s8]). The charter usually gives the investors the veto instead | As the agreement says |
| Can duties or liability be limited? | Director money liability can be limited, with listed exceptions ([7-102-102(2)(d)][t7]). Officers: no such clause found | Directors and officers, with listed exceptions ([102(b)(7)][d8s1]). Section 144, rewritten in 2025, gives controlling holders safe harbours ([144][d8s4]) | Restricted or eliminated if not manifestly unreasonable; good faith can't be eliminated ([7-80-108][t7]) |
| Who pays the founder's lawyer while the case runs? | The company may advance costs on a written affirmation and undertaking, and may promise to in advance ([7-109-104, -109][t7]). Unless the articles say otherwise, a director can also ask a court, which may order it even if those steps weren't met ([7-109-105][t7]) | Advance on an undertaking; can be made mandatory. The Court of Chancery decides, and may do so summarily ([145(e), (f), (k)][d8s4]) | Reimbursement for ordinary-course liabilities without breach ([7-80-407][t7]). Advancement is for the agreement |
| What must a company say when it buys back a holder's stock? | In a closed corporation, an officer or director buying from a holder must disclose what's material ([Van Schaack][vanschaack]). Colorado's and federal fraud law apply ([11-51-501, -604][t11]; [Rule 10b-5][e10b5]). Repurchases must pass the solvency tests ([7-106-401][t7]) | Fiduciary law is judge-made. Federal fraud law applies, and Colorado's Act for a Colorado sale. Delaware's Act needs a real link to Delaware beyond incorporation ([FdG Logistics][fdg]). Repurchases can't impair capital ([160][d8s5]) | Same fraud law if units are securities, which is judge-made |
| Where's the dispute heard? | A Colorado district court, on the ordinary civil docket. No specialist business court, per the [bar journal][cl-courts] | Court of Chancery, for elections and records ([225, 220][d8s7]) and advancement ([145(k)][d8s4]). A Delaware company in Colorado stays under Delaware law for internal affairs ([7-90-805(4)][t7]) | A Colorado district court |
| Non-compete and wages? | **The same in all three.** For a worker who at termination primarily resided and worked in Colorado, Colorado law governs a non-compete whatever the state of formation ([8-2-113(6)][t8]), and the Colorado wage law follows the work | Same | Same |

**Does Colorado follow Delaware?** No rule says so, and it's mixed in practice. The Colorado Supreme Court has said the legislature
"has consistently relied on" the Model Business Corporation Act when writing corporate law, and it treated that Act's views as
persuasive ([Szaloczi][szaloczi]). In a limited partnership suit brought on the partnership's behalf, it chose New York's review standard over Delaware's
([Hirsch][hirsch]). The Court of Appeals has looked to other states when Colorado had nothing, citing a Delaware opinion among them
([Gagne][gagne]), and has declined a party's invitation to adopt Delaware's entire fairness test, finding Colorado's approach
functionally similar ([Kim][kim]). So Colorado shouldn't be assumed to track Delaware. A Colorado company gets less certainty on a
close question, and it isn't locked into Delaware's answers either.

**All operating companies** (also leaving out investing, financial services, banking, insurance, real estate and construction),
604 companies: Delaware corporation 284 (47%), Colorado LLC 123 (20%), Colorado corporation 95 (16%), Delaware LLC 56 (9%).

**What this shows.** Colorado corporations do raise exempt money, in real numbers, and larger technology raises are overwhelmingly
Delaware corporations. **What it can't show:**

- **Cause.** Founders who expect big rounds choose Delaware, and many convert before filing, so they count as Delaware. The data doesn't
  show that a Colorado charter makes a raise smaller.
- **Who invested.** It doesn't say whether any venture fund bought.
- **All raises.** A private sale that relies only on the Securities Act's private offering exemption files no Form D, so many small rounds aren't here.
- **Final amounts.** First notices come within 15 days of the first sale, so "amount sold" is a floor.

## Converting later

| Route | Votes | State filing fees | Tax |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colorado corporation to Delaware corporation | Board recommends, then a majority in each class entitled to vote ([7-90-201, -202, 7-111-103][t7]). Same entity afterward ([265][d8s9]) | **From $443**: Colorado conversion filing $50, Delaware conversion certificate $184, Delaware incorporation from $109, Colorado foreign authority $100 | Mere change of place ([368(a)(1)(F)][us368]); fit on the facts is for your own tax professional. Qualified small business stock exchanged keeps its clock. A gain limit applies only if the company isn't then a qualified small business ([1202(h)(4)][us1202]) |
| Colorado LLC to Delaware corporation | As the operating agreement says; failing that, **every owner** ([7-90-201.4][t7]) | **From $443** | A contribution for stock ([Rev. Rul. 2004-59][rr2004], [351][us351]). **The clock starts at conversion** ([1202(i)][us1202]) |
| Colorado LLC to Colorado corporation | Same as above ([7-90-201.7][t7]) | $100 combined | Same as above |
| **Lawyer's cost** | **Unknown.** One firm says costly and slow after long operation, with no number ([Wilson Sonsini][wsgr]) | | |

Whether converting out of Colorado triggers appraisal rights is a lawyer point ([7-113-102, 7-90-206][t7]). Colorado's own crowdfunding
exemption needs an issuer organized here ([11-51-308.5(3)(a)][t11]), so conversion gives that up; federal crowdfunding isn't affected. A qualified small
business stock clock already running is the reason to form a corporation early if a fund is a real possibility.

## What couldn't be checked

- **What founders know** about Colorado corporations. No data.
- **How often investors require conversion,** and what a lawyer charges for it. No data.
- **Whether institutional funds invested** in any Colorado corporation in the SEC data.
- **Colorado and Delaware fee amounts** weren't reopened for this comparison. See [the cost section](#cost-to-form-and-keep-up).
- **Case law** was read through passages and summaries from a legal research tool, not full opinions, and later history wasn't fully
  run. A lawyer should cite-check [Polk][polk], [Colt][colt], [Gagne 2014][gagne], [Gagne 2019][gagne2], [Van Schaack][vanschaack], [Szaloczi][szaloczi],
  [Hirsch][hirsch], [Kim][kim] and [FdG Logistics][fdg] before relying on them. A research tool suggests a federal decision may limit how [Colt][colt] applies to
  non-derivative claims; that wasn't confirmed.
- **The "disappearing Delaware effect" study** finds the value effect faded for small firms. Whether those firms are comparable to startups wasn't checked. They may be public companies.
- **Removing a Colorado director by consent** and **board-set series as a route around class votes** are readings of the text. No
  decision was found on either.
- **Delaware's 2025 amendments.** On 2026-02-27, its Supreme Court upheld section 144's safe harbours and their retroactive reach, as recorded in the
  [document map][map]. That decision didn't address section 220. The effect on any company is for a lawyer.
- **Tax.** Have your own tax professional review the conversion. Conversion tax depends on the facts, including debts above basis ([26 U.S.C. 357(c)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t26/s357/)).
